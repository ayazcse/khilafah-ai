Overview of Zakat and Waqf as Economic Institutions

Zakat is a mandatory form of almsgiving on qualifying wealth above a
minimum threshold known as nisab, commonly cited as the value of 85 grams
of gold or 595 grams of silver, at a standard rate of 2.5% for cash, gold,
silver, and trade goods. The specific rulings on what counts as zakatable
wealth, how liabilities are treated, and which nisab standard applies
differ between schools of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh); the platform's
Economics Lab clearly labels these as configurable assumptions, not a
universal ruling.

Waqf is a permanent charitable endowment: once an asset is dedicated as
waqf, it cannot be sold or inherited, and its revenue must fund the stated
charitable purpose indefinitely, administered by a trustee (mutawalli).
Historically, waqf endowments funded a significant share of public
institutions across the Islamic world, including mosques, schools,
hospitals, and public infrastructure, because the inalienability of the
underlying asset made it a durable mechanism for long-term civic funding.
