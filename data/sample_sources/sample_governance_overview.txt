Overview of Early Islamic Administrative Institutions

The early Islamic polity developed several administrative institutions as it
grew from a small community in Medina into a state spanning a large
territory. The diwan, introduced as a formal register under the caliph
'Umar ibn al-Khattab, tracked stipends owed to soldiers and their families
from the public treasury. Over subsequent decades, 'diwan' broadened to
describe entire government bureaus handling land tax, correspondence, and
payroll.

The bayt al-mal, or public treasury, collected revenue from zakat, kharaj
(land tax), and jizya, and disbursed funds for stipends and public works.
Provincial governors, known as wulat, administered distant regions on
behalf of the caliph, combining military command with tax collection and
often leading communal prayer.

The office of qadi (judge) developed as the polity expanded, with judges
appointed to resolve disputes according to emerging Islamic legal norms.
This formalization of judicial practice is closely connected to the
broader development of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) during the eighth and
ninth centuries CE.
